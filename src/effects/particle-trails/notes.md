## How it works

Two ways to give a particle a trail, drawn side by side on the SAME particles (`method: split`: ribbons on the left, accumulation on the right; the particles' heads are the same sprites on both sides). The particles are a 2D overlay system on the Epic 7 framework (`src/gpu/particles/`), stepped at a fixed 60 steps a second. Three scenes: comets on Kepler orbits round a softened attractor (born on an annulus and sent off at the local circular speed by a `hook_emit`, ±15%, so the orbits are ellipses); a sparkler on a Lissajous path, or under the pointer (gravity, drag, Newton cooling from about 2800 K, so each spark goes from white-yellow to red); and embers in a row of three Lamb-Oseen vortices of alternating sense (a `hook_force` drags them towards that air at 2/s). A press anywhere throws sparks; `drive` (scroll or audio) adds up to 60% more particles to the emission, never speed.

**(a) Ribbons** (`trails.ts` `createRibbons`, `record.wgsl`, `ribbon.wgsl`). Every particle has a ring buffer of `points` entries in a storage buffer of the effect's own: (x, y, temperature, age) per point, and a head word per slot: (newest entry, valid count, the seed of the particle that wrote them, the record step). After every simulation step a kernel, dispatched indirectly over the live count the system's `finish` kernel wrote, appends each live particle's position: the framework's `afterStep` callback (`system.advance(encoder, delta, ribbons.steps)`) records it inside the steps' own compute pass, so a frame of four steps is one pass, and the heads are still drawn extrapolated by the leftover part of a step. If the slot's seed has changed (the pool recycled it for a new particle) or a record step was missed, the ribbon starts again from one point, so a recycled slot never joins the old path. The vertex shader expands each live particle's points into a strip: instance _i_ is the _i_-th live particle, vertex _v_ is corner _v_ mod 6 of segment _v_ / 6, with no vertex buffer; segments past the valid count collapse outside the clip volume. Adjacent segments share the offset of their common point (the normal of the averaged tangent), so the strip has no gaps and no overlaps at the joins, which additive blending would show as beads. Width and brightness taper with the point's age (`taper`, brightness as the square of the remaining share), and a ribbon thinner than 0.8 px is drawn at that width and dimmed by the ratio, as the sprite renderer's pixel floor does. The draw is `drawIndirect`: the instance count is copied on the GPU from the system's sprite arguments, never read back. A particle that dies is no longer in the live list, so its ribbon is simply not drawn.

**(b) Accumulation** (`createAccumulation`, `accumulate.wgsl`). A persistent `rgba16float` target the size of the canvas, ping-ponged. Every frame: `decay` multiplies it by `exp(-dt / tau)` (frame-rate independent), the particles are drawn into it as sprites with a weight of `1 - exp(-dt / tau)`, and `composite` writes it into the scene (times `trail gain`). The weight normalises the deposit, so a particle that stands still converges to its own brightness at any frame rate, and the light a moving particle leaves per second is the same at 30 and 144 Hz. Each deposit is stretched behind its particle over the distance it covers in that frame (with its total light kept), so a fast particle leaves a line, not a row of dots. The decay rounds stochastically to a value a half float holds exactly: a GPU that truncates on the write (Apple's do) would otherwise lose up to a half-float step per frame, more often at 144 Hz than at 30 Hz.

## Cost

GPU time: not measured yet with the benchmark harness (`npm run study`); the scorecard has no `gpuMs`.

Indicative readings, Apple M1 Max, 1920 x 1080, headless Chrome, HUD "GPU" span of one frame including the HDR tail, median of three loads (two other agents were using the same GPU, so the spread is wide), vortex scene, 24 ribbon points:

| Particles (average alive) |                Ribbons |           Accumulation |                  Split |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------: | ---------------------: | ---------------------: |
| 1 000                     | 1.72 ms (1.14 to 1.98) | 1.66 ms (1.34 to 1.81) | 1.96 ms (1.24 to 2.28) |
| 10 000                    | 2.29 ms (2.27 to 2.64) | 1.98 ms (1.89 to 2.01) | 2.57 ms (2.54 to 2.64) |
| 100 000                   | 7.04 ms (6.96 to 7.40) | 3.32 ms (3.22 to 3.45) | 6.77 ms (5.97 to 7.02) |

The default (`split`, 1 500) reads about 2 ms; a last check after every change read 2.51 ms (2.08 to 2.73, three loads, heavier contention). Ribbons at 100 000 particles draw 13.8 million vertices a frame and go over Tier B's 6 ms; accumulation at the same count is half of that, most of it the 100 000 deposit sprites and the tail. At 1 000 particles the two methods cannot be told apart through the HUD's floor.

What scales: ribbons cost in proportion to live particles x points (the record kernel is tiny; the vertex work is not), accumulation in proportion to pixels (two full-screen passes at canvas resolution) plus one sprite per particle. At 1k particles the two are within the HUD's floor of each other; the ribbons' vertex count is what grows.

Bundle: not measured.

## Pitfalls

- **A ribbon must not join a recycled slot to the old path.** A slot freed by a death is handed to the next birth, sometimes in the same step. Checking the age (a new particle is younger) fails for very short lives; the record kernel compares the particle's seed, which the framework draws afresh for every birth, and the record step (a gap starts a fresh ribbon too). `tests/epic7-e.test.ts` runs a one-slot pool through a dozen lives.
- **Each step of a frame needs its own record uniform.** The live list swaps every step, and `writeBuffer` calls before a submit all land first: the record uniforms are a ring of 32 slots, like the framework's own.
- **Recording needs every step.** The first version stepped the system one step at a time (`step(encoder, 1)` and a record pass after each), a compute pass a step and no render extrapolation, because the framework ran a frame's steps in one pass with no way in between. Its `afterStep` callback is that way in; only the warm-up still steps one at a time, to move the sparkler along its path before each step.
- **Half-float truncation** made the first accumulation decay depend on the frame rate on Apple GPUs; the stochastic rounding in `decay` fixes it on average (the test holds 30, 60 and 144 Hz within 1% of `exp(-1 / tau)` after a second).
- **Unnormalised deposits** would make trails 2.4 times brighter at 144 Hz than at 60 Hz; the weight `1 - exp(-dt / tau)` keeps the light per second constant.
- **Brightness does not match by default.** An accumulation trail's brightness is the particle's exposure, `2 size / (speed tau)` of its head for a moving one; a ribbon's is set directly. `trail gain` and `ribbon brightness` balance the halves per scene.
- **Flicker.** `low` everywhere (8 s per preset): no flashing area; the busiest cell sees one flash a second in the vortex and sparkler scenes, two with comets, three with `camera motion`, where bright comet heads sweep back and forth across the same cells (the limit is more than three). The whole-frame mean moves by under 1.5% of white.
- **Light page.** Thin orange streaks: they read by hue, but as ink lines. `scorecard.lightPage` is `no`.

## When to use it

Ribbons when the trail belongs to the particle: comets, thrown sparks, a pointer trail, anything with a camera or scroll that moves the view, anything that needs per-particle width or colour along the trail, and counts up to the tens of thousands. Accumulation when there are very many particles and the trail is a look rather than a shape (motion blur of a dense swarm, a long-exposure feel), when the camera never moves, and when memory per particle matters more than a fixed full-screen cost. Tier B: both halves fit A on this machine at 1k particles, ribbons at 100k do not. The velocity stretch of the sprite renderer (`stretch`, `trail`) is the cheapest trail of all and needs no history: use it for streaks shorter than a frame or two of motion.
