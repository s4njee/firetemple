## How it works

Fire as a fluid (stories 6.2 to 6.7): the stable-fluids solver of story 6.1 (`src/gpu/sim/fluid.ts`) carries temperature, fuel and soot, and a combustion layer turns fuel into heat, soot and expanding gas. The layer is a reusable module, `src/gpu/sim/fire-fluid.ts`, which owns the solver's three hooks, the meaning of the dye channels and the emitters; this effect only picks burners, colours and presets and draws the fields (`scene.wgsl`).

**The fields.** The four channels of the solver's dye (`rgba16float`, four times finer than the velocity grid: 512 x 288 texels over 128 x 72 cells at the defaults): r is the temperature T above ambient (a flame body is 2 to 3, a core 4, capped at 5), g the fuel F (unburnt gas), b the soot S, a the burn rate R, the fuel burnt per second in the last step, which is where the reaction zone is.

**One step** (60 a second, at most 3 a frame), in the solver's order:

- _Forces._ Buoyancy is the solver's own: an upward acceleration of `buoyancy` x (T - `sootWeight` x S) (`buoyancyWeights` [1, 0, -0.1, 0]), leaned by `wind`. Then vorticity confinement (the solver's), and `hooks.forces`: procedural turbulence (`fire-fluid-turbulence.wgsl`, below).
- _Expansion_ (`hooks.divergence`, `fire-fluid-expand.wgsl`). Burning gas takes up more room. Nguyen, Fedkiw and Jensen put that into the projection as a jump in velocity across the flame front; on a grid it is a divergence source S = `expansion` x R (1 / s), averaged over each cell's 4 x 4 dye texels with four bilinear taps. The projection then makes div u = S: zero wherever nothing burns, and an outward push at the reaction zone. The solver runs with `open` edges, because a source in a closed box has no solution, and with the bottom edge closed (`closedEdges: ['bottom']`, added to the solver for this): the gas spreads along the floor and leaves through the sides and the top.
- _Projection_ (30 warm-started damped Jacobi iterations), then the dye is advected (MacCormack). The solver's per-channel `dyeDissipation` cools T at `cooling` (convection), thins soot at `soot fade` and leaves fuel alone: fuel only burns.
- _Reaction_ (`hooks.dye`, `fire-fluid-react.wgsl`), per dye texel. Burners (capsules, up to twelve) inject fuel at `fuel rate`, made lumpy by the baked noise rising through them so it comes off in tongues, and hold T at `burner temperature` (glowing logs, a pilot). Torches add fuel and hold T at their own heat, stray embers only hold T. Then fuel above the ignition temperature burns: burnt = F (1 - exp(-k g(T) dt)), g switching on smoothly from exactly 0 at `ignition` over 0.3; F loses it, T gains `heat release` x burnt, S gains `soot yield` x burnt. The step is exact with g frozen, so no rate burns more fuel than there is. Radiation (dT/dt = -0.02 T^4, integrated exactly) caps the hottest cores. Every value is rounded stochastically to an exact half float before it is stored, so slow changes are not lost to Apple's truncating half-float writes.

**Detail** (story 6.5): three ways to keep a 128-cell grid lively, each a parameter. MacCormack advection (the solver's `scheme`; BFECC as well) undoes most of the smoothing of semi-Lagrangian advection; vorticity confinement puts back rotation at the scale of a few cells; and procedural turbulence adds the curl of a noise potential read from the shared baked tile, at a wavelength of `turbulence wavelength` cells (8, just above the grid), weighted by T / (1 + T) so only the hot gas is stirred, rising with the flow. The potential lives at the cell corners, so its discrete curl on the staggered grid is divergence-free to the last bit and the projection removes none of it. The tile is read at the mip level that drops octaves finer than two cells, which the grid could only alias. The comparison is under Cost.

**Emitters** (story 6.7). `burner`: a row of four logs (the default), a strip along the bottom, a ring, a point jet (a gas nozzle that also pushes the gas up), or none. The pointer is a torch through the shared ignition layer (`createPointerIgnition`): press and drag to spray burning fuel, several touches at once, and a drag also pushes the gas along it. With `draw by itself when idle` (the `torch` preset) a scripted torch draws a slow figure of eight until someone presses. Stray embers (`createAmbientIgnition`, landing on the burners) relight a fire that went out, which matters only when the burner temperature is below ignition (`smoky smoulder`). `drive` (scroll or audio through `createExcitation`) raises the fuel rate by up to 40% of its calm level and never touches the clock.

**Drawing** (story 6.6, `scene.wgsl`, one pass at canvas size). T is mapped to kelvin (300 + 520 per unit) and through `blackbody_radiance` (tamed brightness, 7 at 1800 K), faded in between 0.5 and 1.6 units so warm air is invisible; a real temperature field below 1900 K is red to orange with no blue, which the tail's per-channel tonemapper would turn lemon yellow, so `ramp_whiten` pales the bright cores toward white. Gas and chemical palettes map T through the authored ramps instead (`ramp_gas`, copper, sodium, potassium, lithium); the gas flame adds a saturating glow of the reaction zone, its bright inner cone. Soot absorbs (Beer-Lambert, `smoke.wgsl`): it dims the glow behind it, its coverage hides the page, and it is lit by the fire below (from the burners' centre, shadowed by the soot halfway there with the one-tap gradient trick), a dim ambient and its own heat. The dye is read with a cubic B-spline (four bilinear taps): at 1080p a dye texel covers about four pixels, and plain bilinear magnification of a reaction zone one texel thick showed the texel grid as steps along every edge. It is an overlay (`OVERLAY_PARAMS`, luminance alpha): the soot has real coverage, and the flame needs nothing opaque behind it. The first frame is a fire already burning (`seed()`), so a still frame under reduced motion is a picture.

## Cost

GPU time, measured on a quiet GPU (Apple M1 Max, 1920 × 1080, default settings, simulation and tail included): 1.0 ms per frame on Dawn, where the tail alone reads 0.7 ms; and 3.24 ms for one frame in Chrome 155 at 60 fps (median of 3 loads, 3.02 to 3.33), which includes the browser's timing floor. The gap between the two is larger for these effects than for the single-pass ones, because a fluid step is many small dispatches. Both are inside the Tier B budget of 6 ms on this machine; no integrated GPU or phone has been measured. How the cost moves with grid size and pressure solver is in `docs/studies/epic-6.md` (story 6.10). Earlier indicative HUD readings, Chrome, Apple M1 Max, 1920 x 1080, with other agents' work on the GPU (median of three loads): **2.9 to 3.1 ms** at the defaults (2.79, 2.90, 3.23 and 3.04, 3.10, 3.24 ms in two runs), 2.7 ms for `gas burner`, 3.3 ms with 192 velocity cells (dye 768). Inside the Tier B budget (6 ms) on this machine; the stable-fluids validation scene read 3.1 ms. No integrated GPU or phone has been measured, so the tier is provisional.

What can be counted at the defaults (128 x 72 cells, 129 x 73 velocity faces, 512 x 288 dye):

- **Per step, 42 dispatches:** velocity MacCormack 2, curl and its blur 2, forces 1, turbulence 1, expansion 1, divergence 1, Jacobi 30, projection 1 (all on the 9 thousand cells), dye MacCormack 2 and the reaction 1 (on the 147 thousand dye texels). About 800 thousand texel visits a step, of which the dye passes are 440 thousand and by far the heaviest per visit (each traces a path through the velocity and interpolates).
- **Per frame:** the resolve, one display pass at canvas size (four bilinear dye taps and one shadow tap per pixel), then the HDR tail, which at 1080p costs more than the simulation.
- **Memory:** about 4 MB for the solver (three dye textures are 3.5 MB of it), 26 MB in all at 1080p with the tail (the HUD's figure).
- **What scales it:** the simulation scales with `resolution` squared and not with the canvas; the dye is four times the velocity grid on a side. The display and the tail scale with the canvas.

**Detail preservation compared (story 6.5).** The `detail:` presets switch on one technique at a time against the same campfire, from `detail: none` (semi-Lagrangian, no confinement, no turbulence).

The order follows the counts: the HUD's own `dye` pass reads 0.07 ms semi-Lagrangian and 0.22 ms MacCormack. On Dawn (marginal cost of one more frame, `FIRE_FLUID_BENCH=1` in `tests/fire-fluid.test.ts`) every preset read 0.5 to 1.2 ms with run-to-run ranges of about a millisecond while other agents used the GPU, too noisy to rank. **Recommended default: MacCormack for both fields, confinement 4 / s (a fifth of the 14 x damping limit) and turbulence 3.** MacCormack carries most of the look for about 0.3 ms (a quarter more texel visits than the plain step); confinement and turbulence cost about 0.1 to 0.2 ms each because they run on the coarse grid, and each adds motion the other does not. BFECC is not worth its extra dye pass here.

**Against the cheaper fires.** The Epic 4 shader fires read 0.4 to 1.8 ms in Chrome and the Epic 5 simulations 1.0 to 1.5 ms; this reads about 3 ms, two to three times as much. It earns that where the fire has to be a fluid: flames that react to a visitor (a torch spraying burning fuel, gas pushed by a drag), fires that merge and lean into each other, smoke that is real coverage carried by the same flow, motion that never loops, and a temperature and velocity field that later effects can use (obstacles in 6.8, heat haze over the page in 6.9). For a decorative flame that nobody touches, a footer strip or many small flames, `noise-flame`, `flame-strip`, `fireplace`, `gas-jet` and `torch` look as good for a third of the cost and have a fragment-only path.

## Pitfalls

- **Rise speed is what trips the flash analyser.** Fire puffs at about 3 to 4 Hz, and bright tongues detaching from the body make cells of the analyser's grid swing between flame and dark. With a buoyancy of 1.4 the defaults measured over the threshold (five cells flashing four times a second); 0.9 brought it to one to three cells, and the default 0.8 measures one to three of the three and a half allowed (over 8 to 12 s). Turbulence and confinement made no difference; rise speed did. Every preset measures under the threshold; the defaults are `low`, not by a wide margin, so raise `buoyancy` with care.
- **A source in a closed box has no solution**, and `boundary` applied to all four edges. The fire needs a floor and open sides: the solver gained `closedEdges` (a wall on chosen edges of the `open` boundary, including every multigrid level and the confinement fade); with all four closed it reproduces `wall` bit for bit, with none it is the plain `open` path (both tested).
- **Stochastic rounding must not bump an exact value.** `floor(v / ulp + u) * ulp`, as `combust_quantise` in `combustion-step.wgsl` writes it, rounds the sum to f32 first, so a random u within 2^-14 of 1 moves a value that is already a half float up by one step. Here that lit fuel lying exactly at the ignition temperature and broke the fuel-conservation test; the reaction compares u with the remainder instead. The Epic 5 copy has the same flaw (harmless there).
- **Fuel burns only above ignition.** If the burner temperature is below `ignition`, only embers and the torch light it; that is the smoulder. Above it, the flame sits on the burner.
- **Slow burning makes tall flames.** At a burn rate of 7 / s the fuel burnt within a few cells of the logs and the flame was a short sheet; at 2.2 / s it is carried up and burns over a third of the canvas, which is the look of a wood fire. Premixed gas burns fast (10 / s): short flames.
- **A slow torch drag piles fuel up.** At 24 fuel / s the end of a drag became an over-exposed fireball; 10 / s keeps a trail that burns for about a second.
- **A thin reaction zone shows the dye grid.** The burn rate is one or two texels thick; drawn at full contrast with bilinear magnification it showed stair-steps at 1080p. The display reads the dye through a B-spline, the gas colour comes from T through `ramp_gas`, and the reaction glow saturates.
- **The turbulence's finest octaves alias.** The baked tile's octaves reach an eighth of the wavelength, below the grid; it is read at the mip level that leaves about one texel per cell.
- **Flames converge.** A row of burners makes one column, not four: the plume draws air in from both sides (entrainment). That is physics; separate fires need distance or a ring.
- **Dye is not conserved by advection.** MacCormack's limiter keeps it in range but the totals wander by a few percent; the conservation test therefore runs with no velocity at all.
- **Steps in one frame need their own uniforms** (seed and time): the module keeps one buffer per step index.

## When to use it

As the fire that has to be a fluid: interactive heroes and backdrops, burners that are page furniture (a strip along a footer, a ring round an avatar, a gas jet), and fires that wrap round or distort the page (6.8 and 6.9 build on `fire-fluid.ts`). It is Tier B (about 3 ms at 1080p on an M1 Max in Chrome), an overlay that reads over a dark page as light and over a light one as a saturated flame with grey smoke, and it needs compute shaders, so there is no WebGL2 path. Where nobody touches the fire and it only has to look like one, the Epic 4 shader fires are a third of the cost; for fire spreading over a surface, the Epic 5 simulations; for a volume, `fire-3d` (6.11).
